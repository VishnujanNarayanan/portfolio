What my intraday model actually predicted on 9.4M NSE ticks

Quant / ML9 min readPublished August 2026

The test set was 3,100 minute bars, 1,451 of which were "up" — a base rate of 46.8%. Any honest reading of an intraday classifier starts there, because a model that predicts "up" every single time scores 46.8% precision while knowing nothing. My best configuration reached 61.4% precision, which sounds like a real edge until you notice it made that call 70 times out of 3,100 opportunities.

This is a walkthrough of building a next-minute direction model over 9.4 million NSE tick records across 31 NIFTY stocks, and of the two places I nearly fooled myself. Neither of them is the leakage everyone warns you about.

From ticks to minute bars

The raw data is 2.9 million trades and 6.4 million quotes across 31 symbols, one file per symbol. You cannot model on that directly and you would not want to — at the individual-tick level most of what you see is exchange mechanics rather than signal. I loaded the 31 per-symbol files, tagged every record with its company, concatenated them into unified trade and quote frames, then condensed to minute bars by grouping on company and minute and inner-joining the trade and quote aggregates.

That leaves roughly 11,600 labelled OHLC rows with a spread feature. Going from 9.4M ticks to 11,600 rows is not compression, it is a choice about the timescale at which you believe a signal exists — and it is the single most consequential decision in the project, made before any modelling starts.

The target and the split

The label is next-minute direction, built by shifting close price backwards within each company:

for horizon in horizons:                       # [1, 3, 5]
    merged[f'future_price_{horizon}'] = (
        merged.groupby('company')['c'].shift(-horizon))
    merged[f'future_return_{horizon}'] = (
        (merged[f'future_price_{horizon}'] - merged['c']) / merged['c'])

The groupby('company') matters. Shifting the close column without it would pull the first bar of one symbol into the last bar of the previous symbol, which manufactures a label out of two unrelated stocks. That is the leakage bug people write about, and it is genuinely easy to hit.

For the split I held out the last 100 bars per company rather than sampling randomly:

train = merged.groupby('company').apply(lambda x: x.iloc[:-100]).reset_index(drop=True)
test  = merged.groupby('company').apply(lambda x: x.iloc[-100:]).reset_index(drop=True)

31 companies × 100 bars = the 3,100 test rows above. A random split here would be indefensible: adjacent minute bars share rolling-window features, so a randomly chosen test row usually has its own neighbours sitting in the training set. Features are rolling means and standard deviations over 3, 5, 10 and 20-bar windows on close and volume, all computed with groupby('company').transform so no window ever spans two symbols, and all backward-looking so none of them can see the future.

So the obvious traps are handled. The two that actually bit me are subtler.

Trap 1: the threshold was chosen on the test set

A classifier gives you a probability, and you decide where to cut. I swept thresholds and picked the one that scored best:

for threshold in [0.5, 0.6, 0.65, 0.7]:
    preds = (probs > threshold).astype(int)
    precision = precision_score(y_test, preds, zero_division=0)
    print(f"Threshold: {threshold}, Precision: {precision:.4f}, Trades: {preds.sum()}")

At 0.5 the model made 1,149 calls at 47.95% precision — statistically indistinguishable from the 46.8% base rate, which is the number that should have set my expectations for everything that followed. Raising the cut to 0.6 gave 50.8% precision on 327 calls. Pushing higher moved precision below the base rate, so the sweep was not a monotonic quality dial; it was a small sample getting smaller.

The best configuration — a random forest with the cut at 0.63 — reached 61.4% precision. That figure is real in the sense that I did not compute it wrong, and misleading in the sense that I selected the threshold by looking at the test set. The moment you tune anything against your holdout, that holdout stops measuring generalisation and starts measuring how hard you searched. Nothing in the code leaked a future price; the leakage was in my process.

Trap 2: 70 trades is not a result

At threshold 0.63 the confusion matrix is:

Precision at threshold 0.63: 0.6143
Confusion Matrix:
[[1622   27]
 [1408   43]]
Predicted 1 count: 70
Actual 1 count: 1451

70 predictions. 43 right, 27 wrong. Against a 46.8% base rate you would expect about 33 correct by chance, so 43 is roughly two and a half standard deviations up — the kind of number that looks like significance until you remember it is one of several thresholds I tried on one dataset, and that the multiple comparisons make the nominal p-value meaningless. Read the other axis of that matrix and the picture is plainer: 1,408 of 1,451 actual up-minutes were missed. Recall is about 3%.

What I had was not a model that predicts the market 61% of the time. It was a model that abstains on 97.7% of minutes and, on the tiny remainder, has an edge I cannot distinguish from luck at this sample size.

The backtest, and why it settled the question

I simulated fixed-size trades over the test window at the 0.6 threshold's 327 signals. The result was effectively flat before costs — well under a tenth of a percent on the starting capital — and comfortably negative once realistic Indian brokerage and STT are applied to 327 round trips. I am not quoting the figure because it does not clear the bar I set for reporting numbers: it does not beat a naive baseline in any meaningful sense.

That is the useful outcome. The precision number in isolation invites you to keep going. The P&L with costs attached tells you the edge is smaller than the friction, which is the only question that matters and the one a classification report never answers on its own.

What I'd do differently

Split three ways, not two. Threshold and hyperparameters get chosen on a validation slice; the holdout is touched once, at the end, and whatever it says is the answer. My whole threshold sweep should have run against data the final number never saw.

Put costs in the objective, not the postscript. Optimising precision and checking P&L afterwards is backwards when a round trip costs more than the edge. The threshold sweep should have been maximising net return per trade, in which case most of the range would have been visibly negative from the start.

Walk forward instead of one split. One 100-bar-per-symbol holdout is a single sample of one market regime. Rolling the train/test boundary forward and reporting the distribution across windows would say far more than a single precision figure, and would have made the sample-size problem obvious immediately.

Size the holdout to the decision. If a configuration only fires 70 times in the test window, the test window is too short to evaluate it. Either lengthen the data or restrict the search to thresholds that produce enough decisions to measure.

The data pipeline underneath this — the tick ingestion and the minute-bar construction — is the part I would reuse. It is described on the minute-level stock prediction page, and the ingestion patterns it shares with the rest of my market data work are in reruns that converge.

Elsewhere

Marcos López de Prado's Advances in Financial Machine Learning is where purged and embargoed cross-validation comes from — the standard fix for exactly the overlapping-window problem that makes a random split useless here. His chapter on backtest overfitting is a direct description of trap 1.

scikit-learn's TimeSeriesSplit is the minimum viable version of walk-forward validation and takes about four lines to adopt.

On reporting: a precision-recall curve against the base rate would have shown in one picture what my threshold table took several runs to reveal — that most of the curve sits at or under the no-skill line.
