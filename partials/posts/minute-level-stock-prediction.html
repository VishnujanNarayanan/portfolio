I wanted to know whether the price of a stock would be higher or lower one minute from now. I had 9.4 million raw records from the National Stock Exchange to work with, covering 31 large Indian companies. This is what I built, what it scored, and the two ways I nearly convinced myself it worked.

Here is the headline, stated the way it should be stated. On my test data, prices went up in 46.8% of minutes. That is the number a model has to beat, because a program that ignores every input and always guesses "up" is right 46.8% of the time — scikit-learn will build you that program in one line as a DummyClassifier, and every result should be quoted against it. My best setup was right 61.4% of the time — which sounds like a real result until you see that it only made that guess 70 times out of 3,100 opportunities.

Turning 9.4 million records into something you can model

The raw data comes in two kinds. Trades are records of shares actually changing hands — 2.9 million of them. Quotes are the prices buyers and sellers were advertising at a moment in time, whether or not anyone accepted — 6.4 million of those. Together, 9.4 million rows across 31 companies, one file each.

You cannot usefully predict anything from individual records at that level. Most of what you see minute-to-minute at tick resolution is the mechanics of how an exchange matches orders, not information about where the price is going. So the first job is to zoom out: I grouped every record by company and by minute, and collapsed each group into a single row holding the open, high, low and close price for that minute, the total volume traded, and the average gap between buying and selling prices.

That leaves about 11,600 rows. Going from 9.4 million records to 11,600 is not just making the file smaller — it is a decision that you believe something predictable happens on a one-minute timescale and that anything faster is noise. It is the most consequential choice in the whole project, and it gets made before any modelling starts.

Defining the question and holding data back

To teach a model, each row needs an answer attached: did the price go up in the following minute? That means taking the closing price and pulling it backwards by one row, so each minute is labelled with what happened next.

for horizon in horizons:                       # 1, 3 and 5 minutes ahead
    merged[f'future_price_{horizon}'] = (
        merged.groupby('company')['c'].shift(-horizon))
    merged[f'future_return_{horizon}'] = (
        (merged[f'future_price_{horizon}'] - merged['c']) / merged['c'])

The groupby('company') is doing real work there. Without it, pulling the price column backwards would take the first minute of one company's trading day and attach it to the last minute of a different company's — inventing an answer out of two unrelated stocks. This is the classic mistake in this kind of work, and it is easy to make.

Then the data has to be split, so the model can be judged on rows it never saw while learning. I held back the last 100 minutes for each company:

train = merged.groupby('company').apply(lambda x: x.iloc[:-100]).reset_index(drop=True)
test  = merged.groupby('company').apply(lambda x: x.iloc[-100:]).reset_index(drop=True)

31 companies with 100 minutes each is the 3,100 test rows from the opening. Splitting by time rather than at random matters here: neighbouring minutes share features that are calculated from rolling windows of recent history, so a randomly chosen test row would usually have its own immediate neighbours sitting in the training data. The model would effectively have seen the answer.

The features themselves are averages and volatility of price and volume over the previous 3, 5, 10 and 20 minutes, calculated per company so no window ever spans two stocks, and always looking backwards so none can see the future.

So the obvious traps are handled. The two that actually caught me are quieter.

Trap one: I tuned the dial while watching the score

The model does not answer "up" or "down". It produces a number between 0 and 1 for how confident it is, and you choose the level of confidence at which you act. Set it low and you act often on weak signals; set it high and you rarely act but only on strong ones. I tried several levels and kept the one that scored best:

for threshold in [0.5, 0.6, 0.65, 0.7]:
    preds = (probs > threshold).astype(int)
    precision = precision_score(y_test, preds, zero_division=0)
    print(f"Threshold: {threshold}, Precision: {precision:.4f}, Trades: {preds.sum()}")

At the middle setting the model acted 1,149 times and was right 47.95% of the time — effectively identical to the 46.8% you get by always guessing "up", which is the reading that should carry the most weight in the whole table. Raising the confidence level to 0.6 gave 50.8% over 327 attempts. Pushing higher made it worse than always guessing up, so this was never a quality dial being turned up; it was a shrinking sample.

The best result, 61.4%, came from a random forest with the level set at 0.63. That figure is arithmetically correct and still misleading, because I chose 0.63 by looking at how it scored on the very data I was using to judge it. Held-back data only measures whether a model generalises for as long as you make no decisions based on it. The moment you tune anything against it, it starts measuring how many things you tried. Nothing in my code leaked a future price — the leak was in my process.

Trap two: 70 guesses is not evidence

Here is the full result at that setting:

Precision at threshold 0.63: 0.6143
Confusion Matrix:
[[1622   27]
 [1408   43]]
Predicted 1 count: 70
Actual 1 count: 1451

The model committed 70 times out of 3,100 minutes. It was right 43 times and wrong 27. If it had no skill at all you would expect about 33 right out of 70, so 43 is high — but with numbers this small that is roughly the margin you would get from coin flips going your way, and I had already tried several confidence levels, which makes finding one good-looking result much less impressive than it sounds.

The other direction of that grid is more damning. There were 1,451 minutes where the price actually rose, and the model stayed silent for 1,408 of them. It catches about 3% of what it is looking for. What I had was not a model that predicts the market 61% of the time; it was a model that declines to answer 97.7% of the time and, on the small remainder, has an edge I cannot tell apart from luck.

The test that settled it

A percentage is easy to admire, so I simulated actually trading it — fixed position size, every signal at the 0.6 setting, 327 trades across the test period. The result was effectively flat before costs, and clearly negative once realistic Indian brokerage and transaction taxes are applied to 327 round trips. I am not quoting the figure, because it does not beat doing nothing and I said I would only put numbers on results that clear that bar.

That is the useful part. A precision score in isolation invites you to keep tuning. Money with costs attached tells you the edge is smaller than the friction — which is the only question that matters, and one an accuracy figure will never answer on its own.

What I would do differently

Split the data three ways, not two. One slice to learn from, a second to make choices like the confidence level, and a third that is opened exactly once at the end. Every bit of tuning I did should have happened against data the final number never saw.

Judge on money from the start. Optimising for accuracy and checking the profit afterwards is backwards when a single round trip costs more than the edge. Had I scored each confidence level on net return instead, most of the range would have been visibly underwater from the first run.

Test on many periods, not one. TimeSeriesSplit does this in about four lines. A single held-back window is one sample of one market mood. Sliding the train/test boundary forward through the data and reporting the spread of results across all those windows says far more than one number, and would have made the sample-size problem obvious straight away.

Match the test period to how often the model acts. If a setting only fires 70 times across the whole test window, the window is too short to judge it. Either use more data or restrict yourself to settings that produce enough decisions to measure.

The data pipeline underneath this is the part I would reuse without changes. It is described on the minute-level stock prediction page, and the ingestion approach it shares with my other market data work is in making pipeline reruns converge.

References and further reading

Marcos López de Prado's Advances in Financial Machine Learning is the standard reference for testing models on financial data specifically, including the techniques for splitting time series so that overlapping windows cannot leak answers between the two halves. His chapters on how backtests mislead describe trap one almost exactly.

Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu's The Probability of Backtest Overfitting puts numbers on the thing I did by hand: try enough configurations against one held-out period and a good-looking result is the expected outcome rather than evidence. It is the paper to read if you have ever tuned a threshold while watching the score.

The specific tools

  • DummyClassifier — the baseline in one line. If your model does not clearly beat it, there is nothing to discuss.
  • TimeSeriesSplit — testing across many periods instead of one, which is the smallest fix for the sample-size problem here.
  • Precision-recall curve — plotted against the 46.8% baseline it shows in one picture what my table of confidence levels took several runs to reveal: most of the curve sits at or below the line you get for free.
  • scikit-learn on cross-validation for time series — why a random split leaks when neighbouring rows share rolling-window features.
